Model notes & what is not modeled

  • Eclipse: circular orbit, fraction in shadow ⟦⟦f_e = frac{1}{π} acos frac{sqrt{h^2 + 2 R_E h}}{(R_E + h) cos β}⟧⟧ β = 0° is the worst case (sun in orbit plane); β = 90° is a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit with no eclipse (only achievable near-polar, and the β drifts seasonally).
  • Array: sized to carry the IT load plus battery recharge during sunlight: ⟦⟦P_array = frac{P}{η_pcu} (1 + frac{f_e}{f_d η_rt})⟧⟧ Area uses 1361 W/m² × cell efficiency × derate (pointing, temperature, packing, end-of-life degradation).
  • Thermal: every watt of electrical power ends up as heat. Two-sided radiator: ⟦⟦A_rad = frac{Q}{2 ε σ (T_rad^4 − T_sink^4)}⟧⟧ The effective sink in LEO is not 3 K: Earth IR (~240 W/m²) and albedo load a radiator, so 200–250 K is typical. ⟦T_rad = T_chip − ΔT⟧: the radiator can only be as hot as the chip junction minus the drop through cold plate, coolant loop and fin, so an 85 °C chip with a 30 K drop gives a 328 K radiator. Hotter-qualified silicon is the main way to shrink the radiator.
  • Drag: ⟦F_D = ½ ρ v^2 C_d A_proj⟧ with an exponential-interpolated standard atmosphere (mean solar activity) scaled by the solar-activity multiplier. Projected area = fraction × (array + radiator planform); sun-tracking arrays in a terminator orbit can fly nearly edge-on, in other orbits the orbit-averaged projection is large.
  • Propulsion: electric thrusters cancelling drag continuously: jet power ⟦P_thr = frac{F_D v_e}{2 η_thr}⟧ with ⟦v_e = I_sp g_0⟧; propellant rate ⟦ṁ = F_D / v_e⟧. Decay lifetime integrates ⟦frac{d a}{d t} = −ρ frac{C_d A}{m} sqrt{μ a}⟧ from the design altitude down to 150 km.
  • Launch: ideal energy per kg ⟦ε = frac{μ}{R_E} − frac{μ}{2 r}⟧ (no rotation credit, no losses). Chemical energy per launch assumes a Starship-class stack burning ≈1000 t of methane (≈50 MJ/kg LHV). Payload per launch is not derated for altitude or inclination.
  • Economics: capex (compute hardware + spacecraft hardware + launch) is annualized with a capital-recovery factor over the design life. Upkeep = propellant resupply (charged at launch $/kg; the propellant itself is negligible), replacement of failed compute units (hardware + relaunch, since nothing is repairable on orbit) and operations as a share of capex. The ground baseline uses the same hardware cost and life, a 15-year facility, PUE-scaled electricity, and a 2 %/yr repair allowance. Orbit has no electricity bill — its power plant is in the spacecraft hardware and launch lines.
  • Politics & permitting: the direct political bill (lobbying, PACs, community benefit agreements) is a recurring % of facility capex; the whole US industry's known political money is ≈0.1 % of its capex, so this line cannot move the verdict — it is here so you can see that. Tax incentives are the larger, opposite-signed political term and are taken off ground hardware, facility and electricity. The cost that does matter is time: expected ground time-to-power ⟦t_power = t_permit (1 + frac{p}{2 (1 − p)})⟧ for block probability ⟦p⟧, charged as carrying cost on half the facility capex plus 8 % sunk cost per blocked attempt. Orbit time-to-power = licensing lead time + fleet launches ÷ cadence. Compute delivered ⟦D⟧ years late is worth ⟦(1 − x)^D⟧ of compute delivered now, so delivering the same value after waiting ⟦D⟧ years costs ⟦(1 − x)^{−D}⟧ as much; the excess, ⟦C_time = C_yr ((1 − x)^{−D} − 1)⟧, is booked on each side as the opportunity cost of time to power. It is part of annual cost and LCOC, so the Investor's verdict prices time.
  • Verdicts: commercial = levelized cost per IT-kWh, orbit vs ground, with "marginal" for anything within ±15 % (well inside the uncertainty of these inputs). Environmental = lifetime CO₂e per IT-kWh with the same band; water is reported but orbit wins it by construction (no cooling towers, no power plant), and debris, re-entry aerosols and night-sky impacts are listed but not scored. There is no separate permitting verdict: time to power enters the commercial verdict as the opportunity-cost line, which is the only place where politics changes the answer; set value decay to 0 to see the plain bill. Neither verdict prices latency, sovereignty, insurance availability, or debris liability.
  • Environment: launch CO₂ = methane burned × 2.75 (stoichiometry) × a lifecycle multiplier (upstream leakage, LOX/LNG production) × an optional high-altitude forcing multiplier; it is prorated by mass share of each launch. Embodied carbon is per kW of IT (hardware), per kg (spacecraft) and per kW over a 15-year life (facility). Ground water = on-site WUE + power-plant consumption × PUE; both sides carry fab water for the chips.
  • Offsets: both sides may buy credits for the same share of their annual CO₂e at one price; the bill goes into annual cost and LCOC. Only share × integrity is netted off the reported intensity, so cheap low-integrity credits barely move the cost and move the verdict less. Because both sides offset the same share, the orbit/ground ratio never changes; only the "neutralised" state (100 % share at 100 % integrity) retires the Planet's verdict, and the comparison becomes purely a bill. The compliance carbon price is a separate slider: a datacenter in the EU pays ≈€82/t through its power whether or not it buys offsets. The high-altitude forcing multiplier is not CO₂ (soot, water vapour and NOx are short-lived), so "offsetting" it with CO₂ removal is GWP bookkeeping, not physics. Debris, re-entry aerosols, night sky and water cannot be offset and stay unscored.
  • Not modeled: radiation hardening and single-event upsets (and the mass of shielding), micrometeoroid/debris protection, deployable-structure dynamics at km² scale, array shadowing of radiators, Earth-link bandwidth (laser downlink), on-orbit servicing or repair (failed units are written off and replaced, never fixed), payload derating for altitude or inclination, collision risk and conjunction avoidance, and end-of-life disposal.
